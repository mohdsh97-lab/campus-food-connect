Our cause

A meal should never be an afterthought.

The case for connecting food waste prevention with student support.

Imagine sitting in class while wondering whether you can afford dinner. Now imagine safe, unserved food being discarded after an event. These situations do not cancel each other out automatically. They show why a campus needs an connection between food and people who need it. Campus Food Connect proposes that connection, combining student support with food waste prevention. We believe a meal should offer dignity, and a useful resource deserves a better destination than a trash bin.

Research shows that unreliable access to food is a concern among college students. Nikolaus et al. (2020) reviewed evidence from 51 study samples and found variation in reported food insecurity. Differences in survey methods and sampling limit comparisons. We should therefore avoid presenting one percentage as the exact rate on our campus. The responsible conclusion is that student food insecurity deserves attention and that local needs should be assessed. Listening to students matters as much as reading national research.

Waste also needs more than a catchy message. Ellison et al. (2019) evaluated a university dining hall campaign and found that its effect on waste behavior was not statistically significant, although students’ beliefs changed. This finding challenges the idea that awareness alone will solve the problem. Our proposal combines education with practical coordination: identify suitable surplus food, arrange collection through approved partners, and communicate about opportunities to help. We would measure results rather than assume that good intentions produce impact.

Practical changes can make a difference. Richardson et al. (2021) found less plate waste with smaller oval platters than with larger round plates in one university dining setting. The study supports testing changes, while also acknowledging questions about nutrition and whether effects continue over time. Campus Food Connect would encourage students to take appropriate portions and dining partners to consider evidence when reviewing waste prevention. Preventing waste and recovering surplus are related goals, but plate leftovers would never be treated as food for redistribution.

Our proposed organization would complement existing food pantries and student assistance programs. We would seek their guidance before planning any distribution, because food safety, reliable scheduling, and respectful access are important. Trained partners would determine whether food is suitable and how it should be handled. Students should not have to share hardship publicly to deserve respect. Surplus meals would provide additional support, not replace broader efforts to address affordability or guarantee that every student’s needs are met.

Supporting this cause means choosing a realistic first step. Students can share the research, ask classmates what support would help, and express interest in volunteering. Restaurants and dining staff can discuss potential partnerships with established assistance providers. Faculty can connect student organizers with relevant campus offices. Together, we can develop a proposal grounded in evidence and shaped by people’s experiences. Campus Food Connect is a student project, not an operating charity. Help us turn concern into a plan: waste less, strengthen connections, and make access to meals a campus priority. A stronger community starts when we treat nourishment as part of student success.
